Elasticity: Resources Follow Demand

One day of a teaching platform during the lockdown. Thousands of students log in whenever a lecture starts and upload their solutions right before the deadline. Can the platform keep up? That depends on who runs it, and on how quickly servers can be added and removed. Whoever runs it, the users are the same, minute by minute.

What is elasticity?

Elasticity is “the degree to which a system is able to adapt to workload changes by provisioning and de-provisioning resources in an autonomic manner, such that at each point in time the available resources match the current demand as closely as possible.” (Herbst, Kounev and Reussner, ICAC 2013)

In the chart, the red area shows under-provisioning, too few resources: users wait longer than the service level agreement (SLA) allows, here 42 ms per request. The gray area shows over-provisioning, too many resources: you pay for servers that nobody needs. The smaller both areas stay without anyone stepping in, the more elastic the platform.

The result at 18:00 sums up the day: the minutes in which the platform was too slow, the server-hours paid (every App server and every unit of the DBMS server that runs or starts), and how many of them nobody needed. Unlike the gray area, which compares the demand with the narrowest tier only, the last number also counts the spare capacity of the other tier. A fixed setup for a normal day has fewer server-hours that nobody needs than the scaling models, but only because it is too small for about half of the day; elasticity saves money compared with buying for the peak.

The load of a tier is the number of users it is serving, divided by the number it can serve within the SLA; for the bottleneck, it counts all users whose requests reach it, including those in its queue. Above 100%, the queue in front of it grows, and the requests in that queue take longer than the SLA allows.

Bottlenecks show up one after another. Every request travels through all tiers and waits for the answers of the tiers below. So the narrowest tier limits all others: the tiers below it receive only the requests it passes on, and the tiers above it are busy with only as many requests as it can answer, while the rest wait in its queue. Only once the bottleneck gets more capacity do the other tiers see the full load.

The more layers the vendor manages, the more automatic the scaling: new hardware on premise takes weeks, a rented physical server hours to days, a virtual machine (IaaS) minutes, but someone has to start it. With PaaS and SaaS, an autoscaler adds and removes servers on its own.

Scaling that only reacts adds servers after the load has risen; when the load rises steeply, as at the start of a lecture, they come too late. Many peaks repeat, though, like the lectures of a timetable or a deadline, so they can be planned for. Scheduled scaling (by a timetable) or predictive scaling (by a forecast from past days) starts servers ahead of time; reactive scaling corrects what the plan gets wrong. Here, the plan expects too few users at 10:15, but the spare capacity of the 70% target takes the extra ones. Planning ahead trades minutes in which users wait (red) for server-hours nobody needs (gray): servers that run before the users come.

In the stack of layers, “optional” marks the layers Virtualization and OS (the operating system of the virtual machines); a platform on physical servers (on premise or hosting) may leave both out. Host OS is the operating system of the physical server.

Assumptions

  • Within the SLA, one App server serves 600 users, and one unit of the DBMS server (a share of its CPU, memory and disk) serves 1,100 users. The App scales out (more servers, up to 8); the DBMS scales up (a larger server, up to 4 units).
  • Automatic scaling aims at 70% load, so it keeps about 30% spare capacity on purpose, for growth while new servers start: above 85% it adds capacity, below 35% it removes it. PaaS adds capacity after 2 minutes above 85%, and it is ready in 3 minutes; it removes capacity after 10 minutes below 35%. SaaS adds capacity after 1 minute from a pool of servers that many customers share, so it is ready within a minute; it removes capacity after 5 minutes.
  • PaaS + timetable scales like PaaS and also follows the timetable you give it: from 20 minutes before each lecture (08:15, 10:15, 14:15) and before the deadline (16:00) until they are over, it keeps enough servers for last Monday's users (2,400, 1,800, 1,700 and 3,000) at 70% load. This Monday, 2,050 users come to the lecture at 10:15, more than planned; the spare capacity of the 70% target takes them. Between two windows less than 10 minutes apart, the servers keep running.
  • With IaaS, monitoring tells an admin after 5 minutes above 85% or after an hour below 35%. The admin acts 15 minutes later and, like the autoscalers, sizes the tier for 70% load; a new or larger virtual machine starts in 3 minutes.
  • A server or unit that is starting costs as much as one that is running; a unit of the DBMS server costs as much as an App server.

Simplifications

  • Here, n App servers serve n times as many users, and so does a DBMS server with n units. In practice, coordinating servers costs extra. The DBMS scales up here because adding a DBMS server is much harder than adding an App server: the data has to be copied and kept consistent.
  • Every bottleneck is fixed here by adding capacity. Often a better query, an index or fewer queries fix it more cheaply: elasticity pays for inefficiency, it does not remove it.
  • Real IaaS clouds offer autoscalers as well; here, IaaS stands for scaling by hand.
  • Every vendor scales well here. In practice, a service is only as elastic as its vendor makes it.
  • The DBMS server grows and shrinks without downtime, and the demand curve is made up.
